# What I Learned — Customer Segmentation & RFM Analysis

1. **RFM segmentation is simple but powerful when the segments are defined transparently.** Quintile-based RFM scoring with rule-based segments (Champions, At Risk-High Value, Potential Loyalists, etc.) produces segments that are explainable to stakeholders — "R≥4, F≥4, M≥4 = Champion" is something a business user can understand and act on. I learned that transparency in segmentation rules is as important as the segmentation itself, because stakeholders need to trust the segments before they act on them.

2. **Customer count and customer value tell different stories.** The At Risk segment has the second-largest number of customers (35), but their revenue (7,391 MAD) is less than half of the Champions' revenue (15,688 MAD from 22 customers). Prioritizing by revenue, not headcount, gives a different picture. I learned that in customer analytics, counting customers can be misleading — the valuable customers are often a small fraction of the base.

3. **The recency quintile is noisier than frequency and monetary.** With 117 customers and a right-skewed recency distribution, the R quintiles have approximately 23 customers each but the boundaries are less meaningful than for F and M (which have clearer distributions). This taught me that the choice of scoring method depends on the distribution of each variable, and that a one-size-fits-all approach (quintiles for all three) is convenient but not optimal.

4. **The segmentation is a snapshot, not a prediction.** RFM describes the current state of the customer base — who was recent, frequent, and monetary. It does not predict which customers will churn, which will become Champions, or what retention offers will work. The At Risk-High Value segment identifies customers who *have been* valuable and are *currently* showing churn signals, but it does not predict that they will actually churn. I learned to be explicit about what RFM can and cannot do.

5. **Reproducibility in customer analytics requires careful data handling.** Generating the dataset with a fixed seed (42) ensures the same customers and transactions every time. But the segmentation thresholds (quintile boundaries) depend on the dataset — if the customer base grows or changes, the thresholds need recalculation. I learned that reproducible analysis is not the same as stable analysis; the results are reproducible for a given dataset, but they are relative to that dataset and need recalibration when the data changes.
