# What I Learned — Manufacturing Quality & Defect Analysis

1. **Segmentation clarifies where to look, but not why.** Breaking down defect rates by machine, product, and operator identified M03 as the highest-defect machine across most products. But the analysis could not explain why M03 has a higher defect rate — that would require downtime data, maintenance history, process settings, and statistical testing beyond what this dataset supports. Learning to stop at "this is where to investigate" rather than overclaiming a root cause was an important discipline.

2. **Statistical significance and practical significance are very different.** The viscosity-defect-rate correlation is statistically significant (p = 0.012) with n = 460, but explains only 1.4% of the variance (R² = 0.014). This is a statistically detectable signal with no operational usefulness. I learned to always report both the p-value and the effect size, and to distinguish "detectable" from "actionable."

3. **Pareto analysis prioritizes by volume, not by rate.** M03 has both the highest defect rate and the highest defect count, so it leads on both metrics. But the Pareto by product showed that Noir Extrême (26.3% of defects) and Noir Gourmand (20.2%) together account for nearly half of all defects, even though their defect rates are not the highest. This taught me that prioritizing by absolute defect count (Pareto) and by defect rate (segmentation) can give different pictures, and both matter.

4. **Cost-of-quality framing turns a quality problem into a business decision.** Without cost data, M03's 37.1% share of defects is just a number. With an illustrative cost per defective unit, it becomes an estimated 32,260–161,300 MAD in losses, depending on the assumption. This makes the investigation priority quantifiable and comparable against other operational demands. I learned that cost framing is what turns descriptive analysis into decision-relevant analysis.

5. **Synthetic data lets me demonstrate the workflow, but limits what I can conclude.** The dataset is well-structured and supports KPI construction, segmentation, Pareto analysis, correlation testing, and a cost sensitivity model. But it cannot support root-cause identification, OEE calculation (no downtime data), process capability (no specification limits), or real cost assessment. I learned to be explicit about what the data does and does not support, and to frame each finding as a signal for investigation rather than a conclusion.
