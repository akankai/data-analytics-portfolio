# What I Learned — Inventory & Supply Chain Analysis

1. **The gap between low-stock rate and stockout rate is itself the key finding.** An 82.83% low-stock rate sounds alarming, but the 4.83% stockout rate shows that most low-stock situations do not result in stockouts. The gap between "below reorder point" and "actual stockout" is the more interesting signal — it suggests the replenishment system is working to some degree, but the reorder points may need review. I learned that a single percentage can be misleading; comparing two related metrics tells a more complete story.

2. **ABC analysis quantifies inventory concentration in a way that a simple total cannot.** An inventory value of 1.502M MAD tells you the size of the inventory, but ABC analysis tells you where the value is concentrated: Olive Oil 1L alone is 28.5% of the total, and the top 4 products account for 64.3%. This means working-capital management should focus on a small number of high-value items. I learned that ABC analysis is a prioritization tool, not just a categorization.

3. **Supplier performance and stockout risk may be connected, but the dataset cannot prove it.** Sahara Trading's 76.52% on-time delivery rate is 15.48 pp below the best supplier (Rif Distribution at 92.00%). The stockout-risk products (Canned Peas 400g, Flour 1kg) may or may not be supplied by Sahara Trading — the dataset does not link suppliers to products at the stockout level. I learned to notice potential connections and explicitly flag them as "not proven by this data" rather than assuming the connection exists.

4. **Inventory turnover interpretation depends on the data structure.** The 0.59× turnover ratio is an observed ratio for the dataset period, not an annualized financial KPI. In a real deployment with beginning-and-end inventory balances over a fiscal period, turnover would be calculated differently. I learned to always specify the period and definition when reporting turnover, because "0.59×" means very different things depending on context.

5. **Concentration analysis applies at multiple levels.** Inventory is concentrated in Casablanca (46.5% of value), in Dry Goods (largest category), and in Olive Oil 1L (largest product). Each concentration is a separate risk and a separate management focus. I learned to look for concentration patterns at every level of the data, not just at the top level.
